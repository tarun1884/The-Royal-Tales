    How to Plan an Indian Wedding Budget

    📅 November 2026 👤 Royal Tales Editorial Team ⏱ 8 min read

    Almost every couple starts in the same place: what will this actually cost? There is no single answer, because an Indian wedding budget is driven far more by decisions than by price lists. Here is how to build one that survives contact with reality.

    Start with the number, not the wishlist

    Most budgets fail the same way. A couple collects ideas first, prices them later, and discovers the total somewhere around the third vendor meeting. Reverse it. Agree one figure you are genuinely comfortable spending, before anything is shortlisted.

    That figure should be the total across every function, not just the wedding day. Mehendi, haldi, sangeet, the ceremony and the reception each carry their own venue, catering, decor and logistics.

    • Agree the total before shortlisting anything
    • Count every function, not only the wedding day
    • Decide who is contributing, and how much, in the same conversation
    • Write it down; a number that lives only in conversation drifts

    A workable split

    There is no universal breakdown, but a useful starting frame is to treat venue, catering and decor as the bulk of the budget, with everything else fitted around them. Adjust the proportions to suit your priorities, then hold the total.

    The point of a split is not precision. It is that when one line grows, you can see immediately what has to shrink.

    • Venue and catering — usually the single largest block
    • Decor and production — stage, florals, lighting, draping
    • Photography and film — the only thing you keep afterwards
    • Attire and jewellery — both families, all functions
    • Entertainment and hospitality — music, guest transport, rooms
    • Contingency — a genuine reserve, not a rounding error
    A budget is not a prediction. It is a set of decisions you have agreed to defend.
    — Royal Tales Team

    The costs couples forget

    Overruns rarely come from the big line items, which get negotiated carefully. They come from the small ones that nobody assigned to anyone.

    • Guest transport and airport transfers
    • Room nights for family and out-of-town guests
    • Vendor meals, and accommodation for their teams
    • Overtime charges when a function runs late
    • Generator backup, permissions and venue security deposits
    • Gifting, trousseau packing and return favours
    • GST and service charges, which are frequently quoted separately

    Where the budget is actually decided

    Three decisions move the number more than any amount of negotiating: how many guests you invite, where you hold it, and which season you marry in.

    Guest count is the most powerful of the three because it multiplies through everything else. Every additional guest is catering, seating, hospitality and often a room night. Trimming the list is usually less painful than trimming the celebration itself.

    Season matters more than people expect. Peak wedding dates command peak pricing across every vendor category at once. A date a few weeks either side of the rush can change the arithmetic.

    Protecting the budget once it is set

    A budget holds when someone is responsible for it. That can be one of you, a family member, or a planner, but it has to be a named person with the authority to say no.

    Keep every quotation in one place, in writing, with what is included and what is not. Most disputes we see are not about price. They are about scope that was discussed but never recorded.

    • One person owns the budget and approves variations
    • Every quote in writing, with inclusions and exclusions listed
    • Payment schedule agreed up front, tied to milestones
    • Review the full picture monthly, not only when a bill arrives
